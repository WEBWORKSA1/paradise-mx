Sargassum in Mexico: when, where and how to avoid it

What the seaweed is, which Caribbean beaches get it, the months it peaks, and the seaweed-free alternatives.

What sargassum is

Sargassum is a brown seaweed that floats in large mats on the open Atlantic, held up by small gas-filled bladders. Out at sea it is a vital habitat for fish, crabs and young turtles. Since 2011, however, enormous blooms have formed across the tropical Atlantic, and currents carry part of that mass west into the Caribbean. When it washes ashore in quantity it turns turquoise water brown and, as it rots on the sand, smells of rotten eggs.

Scientists link the blooms to a mix of nutrient run-off from large rivers, dust from Africa, warming seas and changing currents. What matters for travellers is simpler: the amount varies a lot from one year to the next and from one beach to the next, so a general rule of thumb has to be combined with a last-minute check.

When it arrives

On Mexico's Caribbean coast, the typical pattern is:

  • November to February: usually little or none.
  • March: first arrivals in some years.
  • April to August: the main season, with the heaviest landings often in May to July.
  • September and October: tapering off, though some years run late.

Which beaches get it

Sargassum comes in on east-facing beaches that meet the open Caribbean. That means exposure is highest along the long coast from Puerto Morelos through Playa del Carmen and Tulum down to Mahahual and Xcalak. Some places are naturally sheltered:

  • Cancún's north-facing beaches along the top of the Hotel Zone (around Playa Langosta, Playa Tortugas and Playa Caracol) face Isla Mujeres rather than the open sea.
  • Isla Mujeres' Playa Norte and Costa Mujeres, which face the calm channel.
  • Cozumel's west coast, on the leeward side of the island, where most hotels and reefs are.
  • Holbox and the Yucatán north coast, which face the Gulf of Mexico and are less affected.
  • Bacalar, a freshwater lagoon with no seaweed at all.

And the simplest fix: the Pacific coast and Baja never get Atlantic sargassum. Puerto Vallarta, Huatulco, Los Cabos and La Paz are all seaweed-free alternatives.

How to check before you go

  • Look at citizen-science maps and recent beach photos, such as Sargassum Monitoring.
  • Quintana Roo's authorities publish a colour-coded "sargassum traffic light" for the coast; search for the latest one in the week before you fly.
  • Ask your hotel directly for a photo of the beach that morning. Good hotels will send one.
  • Webcams from beach bars and hotels are useful, but remember that crews often rake beaches at dawn, so check later in the day too.

How to plan around it

  1. Travel from November to March if a pristine Caribbean beach is the point of the trip.
  2. In summer, book sheltered spots (Isla Mujeres, Cozumel's west coast, north-facing Cancún) or a hotel with excellent pools.
  3. Mix in places that don't depend on the beach: cenotes, Bacalar's lagoon, Maya sites and the colonial towns.
  4. Choose flexible rates so you can move within the region if one stretch is hit.
  5. Consider the Pacific for May to June beach trips, when it's still dry and seaweed-free.

Is it harmful?

Swimming among fresh floating sargassum is unpleasant rather than dangerous. The concern is large piles rotting on the sand, which release hydrogen sulphide gas. Brief exposure may cause irritation, and people with asthma or other breathing conditions should avoid lingering near heavy, decomposing banks. Beach crews and the Mexican Navy install barriers and remove seaweed during the season, and many resorts clear their beaches daily.
