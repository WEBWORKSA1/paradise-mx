Mexico entry requirements and tourist fees, explained

Passports, the FMM tourist permit, customs, travelling with children and Quintana Roo's Visitax, step by step.

Entering Mexico as a tourist is straightforward for most visitors from North America and Europe. This checklist covers what to carry and what to expect. Requirements change, so confirm with Mexico's immigration institute (INM), your airline and your own government's travel advice before departure.

Passport and visa

You need a valid passport for the whole of your stay; many airlines prefer six months of validity, so renew early if yours is close. Citizens of the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom, the European Union, Australia, Japan and many other countries don't need a visa for tourism. Others may need a Mexican visa, unless they hold certain valid US, Canadian, UK, Schengen or Japanese visas or residency; check the rules for your passport with a Mexican consulate.

The FMM and how many days you get

The FMM (Forma Migratoria Múltiple) is Mexico's tourist entry record. At most major international airports it is now handled digitally: the officer scans your passport and records or stamps the number of days you're allowed to stay. That number is up to 180 days, at the officer's discretion, and it is not always 180. Look at your stamp or record before leaving the desk.

The officer may ask for proof of a return or onward ticket, where you're staying and that you can support yourself. Keep your booking confirmations handy on your phone. If you're arriving by land and travelling beyond the border zone, you'll need to complete an FMM and pay its fee; it can be started online on the INM website.

At the airport

  • Customs: complete the customs declaration if one is requested, collect your bags and press the button at the customs "traffic light". Green means walk through; red means a quick bag inspection. It's random.
  • Arrivals hall: you'll pass desks offering transport, tours and "free" gifts. Many are timeshare sales pitches. Head outside to your pre-booked transfer or the official taxi desk.
  • Money: airport exchange rates are poor. Change only enough for the first taxi, or use a bank ATM.

Travelling with children

Foreign children entering Mexico as tourists need their own passport. Mexican consulates advise that a minor travelling alone or with an adult who isn't their parent should carry a letter of consent from the parents or guardians, and airlines and officers can ask for proof of consent from any adult travelling with a child.

New since 14 November 2026: hotel check-in checks. An amendment to Mexico's General Tourism Law, published in the official gazette on 13 November 2026, requires hotels, resorts and other tourism providers to verify that adults travelling with minors have parental or legal authority. Until enforcement details settle, pack for each child: their passport, a birth certificate showing the parents' names, and, if a parent isn't travelling, a signed consent letter from the absent parent. Grandparents or other adults should carry a consent letter from both parents (notarised, ideally with a Spanish translation). Ask your hotel before you travel which documents it accepts.

Taxes and tourist fees

Mexico's air passenger charges, including the non-resident fee, are normally already included in your airline ticket. Beyond that:

  • Visitax (Quintana Roo). Foreign visitors to Cancún, the Riviera Maya, Tulum, Cozumel, Isla Mujeres, Holbox, Bacalar and the rest of Quintana Roo must pay this state fee once per stay. In 2026 it is roughly MX$285 to MX$295 per person. Pay it in advance on the official state portal, visitax.gob.mx, and save the QR code; checkpoints at Cancún airport now ask for it. In late 2026 some travellers reported being asked for far more than the official rate at airport collection points, so paying online beforehand protects you.
  • Hotel taxes. States and some municipalities add a lodging tax to hotel bills, and Quintana Roo and others have added charges on rentals. These are usually shown at booking or added at checkout.
  • Park and reserve fees. National parks and marine reserves, such as Tulum's beach area, Cozumel's reefs and Isla Espíritu Santo, charge entry or wristband fees, often in cash.

Driving in

Your home driving licence is valid for tourist driving. You must have Mexican liability insurance; US and Canadian policies are generally not recognised. Beyond the border free zone (which includes the Baja peninsula and part of Sonora), a foreign-plated vehicle needs a temporary import permit from Banjercito. Drive in daylight and prefer toll highways (cuotas), which are better maintained.

Health

No vaccinations are required to enter Mexico from North America or Europe. Check your own health authority's recommendations (often hepatitis A and typhoid for travellers). Use repellent in the rainy season, when mosquito-borne illnesses such as dengue are more common, and drink bottled or purified water. Travel insurance that includes medical evacuation is strongly recommended; private hospitals expect payment or a guarantee upfront.
