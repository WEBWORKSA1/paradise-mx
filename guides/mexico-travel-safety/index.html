Practical travel safety in Mexico's beach destinations

Reading official advisories, getting around, money, water, ocean safety and the common tourist scams.

Paradise.mx editorial team. Reviewed 2026-11-27.

Millions of visitors enjoy Mexico's beach destinations every year without problems. Like anywhere, a little local knowledge goes a long way. This guide covers the practical habits that matter most; it isn't a substitute for your government's official advice.

Reading official advisories

The US, Canadian and UK governments rate Mexico state by state, and sometimes city by city, and they don't always agree. Read the entry for the specific state you're visiting, including the notes about which areas are exempt or restricted, and check again shortly before you fly, because levels change. Every destination page on Paradise.mx links to these advisories.

Getting around

  • From the airport: use a pre-booked transfer or the authorised taxi desks inside the terminal. Agree the fare before you get in.
  • Taxis: many resort towns use fixed zone fares rather than meters; ask your hotel what a fair price is. App-based rides operate in some cities but are restricted at some airports and resort areas.
  • Buses: first-class coach lines such as ADO are comfortable, punctual and widely used by visitors between cities.
  • Driving: carry Mexican liability insurance, drive in daylight, use toll highways (cuotas) where possible, and slow down for speed bumps (topes), which are everywhere.
  • At night: stick to busy, well-lit areas and take a taxi rather than walking long distances.

Money and cards

  • Use ATMs inside banks, malls or hotels rather than standalone street machines, and cover the keypad.
  • Decline "dynamic currency conversion" and pay in pesos.
  • Keep a small amount of cash in your pocket and the rest in the room safe.
  • At petrol stations, check the pump is at zero before filling and count your change.

Ocean and sun

  • Beach flags: green means calm, yellow means caution, red means don't swim, and black means the beach is closed to swimming. Take them seriously.
  • Rip currents are the biggest hazard on open Pacific beaches. In Los Cabos, many beaches, including the famous Divorce Beach, are unsafe to swim at any time. Swim where locals swim and lifeguards watch.
  • Sun: the tropical sun burns fast, even on cloudy days. Use mineral sunscreen, a rash guard and a hat, and drink water.
  • Water: drink bottled or purified water. Most hotels and good restaurants use purified water and ice.

Storm season

Hurricane season runs from June to November in the Atlantic and mid-May to November in the eastern Pacific, peaking in September. If you travel then, buy travel insurance that covers trip interruption for storms, follow the US National Hurricane Center and Mexico's national weather service, and register your trip with your embassy's traveller programme. Resorts in hurricane zones have well-practised plans.

Common scams

  • The arrivals-hall pitch: people in official-looking uniforms offering transport, tours or "free" gifts are often selling timeshare presentations. Walk past to your booked transfer.
  • Fee overcharging: pay Quintana Roo's Visitax online at the official portal before you travel. In 2026, travellers reported being asked for more than the official rate at airport collection points.
  • "Your card didn't work": keep your card in sight when paying.
  • Fake tour sellers: book tours through your hotel, a known operator or a reputable platform, and be sceptical of deals that are far below normal prices.

Getting help

Dial 911 for emergencies anywhere in Mexico. 078 connects to the national tourist assistance line, which includes the Ángeles Verdes highway assistance service. Save your embassy or consulate's number before you go, carry a photocopy of your passport separate from the original, and keep your travel-insurance emergency number in your phone.
