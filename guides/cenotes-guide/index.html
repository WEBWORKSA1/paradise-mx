A first-timer's guide to cenotes

Types of cenotes, where the best ones are, entry fees, etiquette and safety in the Yucatán Peninsula's sinkholes.

What a cenote is

The Yucatán Peninsula is a slab of porous limestone with almost no rivers on the surface. Rainwater soaks through the rock and flows underground through one of the largest flooded cave networks on Earth. Where the roof of a cave collapses, it opens a window onto that water: a cenote (say-NO-tay), from the Maya word ts'onot. There are thousands across the peninsula.

For the ancient Maya, cenotes were the main source of fresh water and sacred places, seen as doorways to the underworld. Chichén Itzá grew up around its Sacred Cenote. Today they are some of the most beautiful swimming spots in the world: cool, very clear freshwater, often lit by shafts of sunlight and framed by roots and stalactites.

Three kinds of cenote

  • Open cenotes look like lagoons or ponds whose cave roof collapsed long ago. They're bright, warm and easy for families. Examples: Cenote Azul near Playa del Carmen, Cenote Azul in Bacalar.
  • Semi-open cenotes are partly roofed, with a hole in the ceiling that lets in a beam of light. They're the most photogenic. Examples: Ik Kil near Chichén Itzá and Suytun near Valladolid.
  • Cave cenotes are mostly or fully underground, reached by stairs or a ladder. They're stunning for snorkelling with a guide and world-famous for cave diving. Example: Dos Ojos near Tulum.

Where to find the best ones

Tulum

Gran Cenote, Dos Ojos, Calavera and Car Wash (Aktun Ha) are a short drive or bike ride from town. Gran Cenote is a favourite for snorkelling with turtles in its open section. Tulum guide.

Puerto Morelos: the Ruta de los Cenotes

A road heading inland from Puerto Morelos passes a string of jungle cenotes, many run by local families, with zip lines and rope swings at some. It is quieter than Tulum and close to Cancún. Puerto Morelos guide.

Valladolid

Cenote Zací sits right in town, and Suytun, Samulá and X'Keken are a few minutes away. Pair them with Chichén Itzá or Ek Balam. Valladolid guide.

Homún and Cuzamá, near Mérida

A cluster of community-run cenotes in villages south-east of Mérida, some reached by horse-drawn carts on old railway tracks. A great full-day trip. Mérida guide.

Visiting: fees, timing and what to bring

  • Fees: most cenotes charge entry, from about MX$100 at small community-run sites to several hundred pesos at famous ones. Many take cash only. Snorkel gear and life jackets may cost extra.
  • Timing: go when they open. You'll have the water to yourself before the tour buses, and morning light is best in semi-open cenotes. Midday sun shafts are spectacular in some, such as Suytun.
  • Bring: swimsuit, quick-dry towel, water shoes for slippery steps, cash in small notes, and a waterproof phone pouch. Leave the sunscreen behind.
  • Water temperature: usually around 24-25°C. It feels cool after the beach, which is the point.

Etiquette that protects them

Cenotes are connected to the same underground water that people drink, and their ecosystems are fragile. Most sites ask you to:

  1. Shower before entering to rinse off sunscreen, repellent, lotion and make-up.
  2. Skip sunscreen and repellent before you swim, even "reef-safe" ones.
  3. Never touch stalactites or rock formations. They take thousands of years to grow.
  4. Wear the life jacket where it's required. It's often a condition of entry.
  5. Take all rubbish with you and respect areas that are roped off.

Safety

Cenotes can be deep, and their edges and stairs are slippery. Don't jump from platforms that aren't designated for it, and check depth first. Never swim into overhead cave sections without a certified guide: cave diving requires specialist training, and even experienced open-water divers die every year by entering caves unprepared. If you're unsure of your swimming, choose an open cenote and keep the life jacket on.
